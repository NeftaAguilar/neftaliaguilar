I started writing at the end of August — <Stat name="firstPostDate" />, to be exact.

Not to build an audience. To force myself to understand things properly. The fastest way I know to find the holes in what I think I know is to try to explain it to someone else: the moment you write "and therefore" and can't finish the sentence, you've found the thing you didn't actually understand.

So far that's <Stat name="posts" /> posts, <Stat name="impressions" /> impressions and <Stat name="reactions" /> reactions. This post is the receipt. Every number in it reads live from [Buffer's API](https://developers.buffer.com) on the server, refreshed once a day — so it will contradict me the moment the data does.

<Stat name="posts" /> posts is not a sample size. It's a start. But the patterns are already sharp enough to have changed how I publish.

## The network matters more than the post

Every piece goes to both networks the same day, minutes apart — sometimes word for word, more often rewritten to fit each one. Whatever separates these bars, it isn't the idea.

<Stat name="widestNetwork" /> shows my posts to roughly <Stat name="reachRatio" /> more people than <Stat name="narrowestNetwork" /> does. Same week, same argument, same person writing it. The distribution is doing more work than the prose.

## Reach and engagement pull in opposite directions

The obvious read is that one network is simply better. The numbers say something more useful.

<EngagementFigure />

The network that reaches the fewest people — <Stat name="narrowestNetwork" /> — is the one where the people who *do* see a post are about <Stat name="engagementRatio" /> more likely to reply, react, or click. A big room where nobody speaks, and a small one where everybody does.

Read the right-hand side of that chart with suspicion, though. Those engagement rates come from denominators in the dozens. One reply on a post seen 24 times is 8%, and it means far less than the percentage suggests. The shape is real; the precision is not.

## My most-seen post isn't writing at all

Ranked by reach, the post at the top isn't an essay. It's a hiring announcement from <Stat name="topEarlierDate" />.

<ReachRankingFigure />

On its own it was seen <Stat name="topEarlierImpressions" /> times — more than every piece of writing since, combined. It's in a different colour above because it's a different kind of post, and dropping it would have made the chart flatter and less true.

That's the part worth sitting with. Reach follows what people need right now — a job, a link, a favour — not how carefully something was written. Which is a decent argument for writing to understand rather than writing to be seen: the first goal I control completely, and it pays out whether or not the second one ever does.
